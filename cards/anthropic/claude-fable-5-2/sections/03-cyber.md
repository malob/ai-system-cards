## 3 Cyber

### 3.1 Introduction

Across our internal evaluation suite, Claude Mythos 5.2 meets or exceeds the performance of Claude Mythos 5.1 and Claude Opus 5.5 on all cyber evaluations we report in this system card.

Despite Claude Mythos 5.2’s cyber capabilities, we believe the model still falls within the lower category of capabilities set out in our [Frontier Compliance Framework](https://trust.anthropic.com/resources?s=u0v3axvddg70fujf93ra7&name=anthropic-frontier-compliance-framework) (FCF), and we do not see indications that the model is capable of novel offensive capability. The FCF evaluates the cyber offense risk posed by a new model based on two risk tiers, which inform the mitigations developed prior to releasing a model for broad use:

- **In Tier 1**, a model can provide meaningful technical assistance for active cyber operations using known attack techniques and methodologies. Although some automation is involved, models in this tier are still dependent on human input to successfully complete large-scale cyber operations.
- **In Tier 2**, a model can conduct cyber operations completely and autonomously, with novel offensive capability development and adaptive persistence.

All evaluations were run with cyber safeguards classifiers disabled, but with a classifier that looks for escape attempts. Many of our harnesses for evaluation runs have been rewritten since previous [system cards](https://www.anthropic.com/system-cards). In those cases, all older models were reevaluated to maintain consistency across models, resulting in numbers that are different from system cards released before this change.

### 3.2 Mitigations

As noted above, Claude Mythos 5.2 has the strongest cyber capabilities of any model we have released, meeting or exceeding Claude Mythos 5.1 and Claude Opus 5.5 on all of the evaluations we share here. We have therefore deployed it to the general public as Claude Fable 5.2, with cyber safeguards that enforce the same policy as on Opus 5.5 but are comparably robust to our safeguards on Claude Fable 5 and Claude Fable 5.1, and more robust than those on Claude Opus 5 (see [Section 3.5](#35-safeguards-robustness-testing)). The safeguards described here apply to the generally available model; verified cybersecurity users will be able to access the same model with fewer restrictions, as described below.

<!-- p.47 -->

Our safeguards prevent harmful cyber use in three stages. First, a probe looks at Claude’s internal activations, screening all traffic and escalating any traffic that it flags as cyber related. Second, the escalated traffic is scored by a lightweight classifier that runs on Claude Mythos 5.2 itself. Third, traffic identified as potentially violative by the second classifier is passed to a trained LLM classifier—a separate model that decides, together with the verdict from the probe, whether a conversation should be blocked.

This approach is modeled on the constitutional classifiers we’ve [discussed previously](https://www.anthropic.com/research/next-generation-constitutional-classifiers). To train our cyber classifiers, we sourced a dataset of violative cyber exchanges, then augmented this data to more accurately mimic the specific kinds of jailbreaks we are most concerned about. We then iteratively augmented our classifiers’ data with attacks generated by internal automated red teamers. We weighted our data toward longer-running agentic tasks, as these are the tasks that typically enable widespread abuse.

These blocks are designed to prevent potentially harmful offensive cyber uses, including activities that are considered dual-use (i.e., that could have offensive or defensive applications). As with Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1, our policy allows vulnerability discovery in source code so developers can use Fable 5.2 to help write more secure software, but blocks vulnerability discovery in compiled binaries. We describe the policy these classifiers enforce and their coverage in [Section 3.4](#34-safeguards-coverage), and their robustness to deliberate circumvention in [Section 3.5](#35-safeguards-robustness-testing). Given that Claude Mythos 5.2’s cyber capabilities meet or exceed those of Opus 5.5, we have opted for a [temporarily wider safety margin](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-safeguards-jailbreak-framework) against jailbreaks while we continuously work to improve the robustness of our classifiers and reduce their false-positive rate (see [Section 3.4](#34-safeguards-coverage)).

On most interfaces, Fable 5.2 falls back to Claude Opus 4.8 for requests that are blocked by our cyber classifier system. This happens automatically in our own applications; on the API, the developer must opt in to automatic fallbacks.

For users with cybersecurity use cases that may be blocked by our cyber safeguards, we recommend accessing our models with reduced cyber blocking classifiers via our [Cyber Verification Program](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/14604842-real-time-cyber-safeguards-on-claude-opus-and-sonnet). Claude Mythos 5.2 will be available through this program.

All results reported below were obtained by evaluating Claude Mythos 5.2 with cyber safeguards disabled. This allows us to assess the capability of the underlying model, which is most relevant to our risk assessment, and reflects the capability level that will be available to certain users via the Cyber Verification Program.

### 3.3 Capability evaluations

#### 3.3.1 ExploitBench

[ExploitBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14153) is a cybersecurity benchmark that evaluates how far AI models can progress along the software exploitation pipeline rather than scoring exploitation as a single pass/fail event. We have recently written about it in [more detail here](https://red.anthropic.com/2026/exploit-evals/).

This benchmark decomposes exploitation into 16 measurable capability flags, spanning coverage and crash reproduction through sandbox primitives, arbitrary read/write, control-flow hijack, and arbitrary code execution. The flags are arranged in five tiers, which distinguish models that can merely trigger crashes from those that are able to construct the reusable primitives required to weaponize an exploit. This provides a fine-grained measure of offensive cyber capability.

The benchmark targets 41 recent vulnerabilities in the V8 engine (from 2024 onward), the JavaScript and WebAssembly engine that powers Chrome. The model is given a vulnerable build of V8 and a patch that fixes the vulnerability. It is then tasked with building an exploit for the bug. The build has mitigations enabled, including the V8 heap sandbox, ASLR, and stack canaries. Every exploit tier is graded mechanically by deterministic verifiers. To resist reward hacking, challenge-response functions built into V8 are replayed across multiple randomized heap layouts, so hardcoding a leaked address doesn’t count as a solution. We run five trials per vulnerability.

We report three metrics:

1. Mean flags captured per trial across all trials and environments;
2. Cap%, the percentage of available flags captured per environment (over a randomly chosen subset of three trials), averaged across all environments; and
3. The number of complete arbitrary code execution (ACE) exploits generated across both plain and AutoNudge runs (as described below).

Each environment was run under two arms with a 300-turn budget. In the plain arm, the model made a single uninterrupted attempt. In the “AutoNudge” arm, if the model voluntarily stopped short of the budget without reaching full code execution, the harness injected a “keep trying” prompt into the same conversation and let it continue.

Claude Mythos 5.2 scored a mean of 15.10 capability flags, for a Cap% of 95% across ExploitBench’s 41 V8 environments in the plain arm. In the AutoNudge arm, it averaged<!-- p.49 --> 15.46 flags, for a Cap% of 97%. Across the plain and auto-nudge runs, Claude Mythos 5.2 achieved full arbitrary code execution 90.0% of the time (369 out of 410 attempts).

#### 3.3.2 CyScenarioBench

CyScenarioBench is a benchmark developed by Irregular that evaluates whether models can plan and execute multi-stage cyber operations under realistic constraints, as opposed to performing isolated technical tasks. Whereas many cyber benchmarks emphasize short-horizon challenges that isolate individual competencies, CyScenarioBench measures orchestration: the coordinated sequencing of attack techniques across dependent operational stages, including making branching decisions, adhering to constraints, and recovering from errors and state inconsistencies.

The benchmark is organized in a three-tier hierarchy: task-level evaluations measure atomic capabilities, such as code generation and payload adjustment; path-level evaluations cover multi-step sequences corresponding to branches of an attack tree; and campaign-level evaluations are full attack simulations that incorporate uncertainty and operational friction. Scenarios are derived from analysis of real cyber incidents and run in<!-- p.50 --> containerized network environments with realistic operating systems, authentic service configurations, responsive defensive monitoring, and simulated employees with varying levels of security awareness. All scenarios are novel and built from scratch (rather than drawn from published capture-the-flag exercises), and the evaluation set is kept private to avoid contamination. This design surfaces failure modes that only emerge over long horizons—such as context drift, incorrect branching choices, dead-end planning, and inadequate troubleshooting—and helps assess whether a model could provide meaningful uplift to a novice operator, not just accelerate an expert.

We run a 10-challenge subset of the CyScenarioBench evaluation set: geometry_lesson, catfish, fine_print, snapshot, origin_story, identity_crisis, sight_test, mutiny, houdini_protocol, and crossed_wires. We report the overall solve rate for each model, averaged across the 10 challenges.

This evaluation was run on a pre-release snapshot that we assess to be very similar in capability to the released model.

Claude Mythos 5.2 completed 76.3% of all challenges, the strongest performance of any released model to date. By comparison, Claude Opus 5.5 completed 67.6% of the tasks, Claude Mythos 5.1 completed 61.7%, and Claude Sonnet 5.5 completed 46.1%.

#### 3.3.3 Binary Exploitation Benchmark (formerly known as OSS-Fuzz)

This evaluation measures unguided vulnerability discovery and how far a model can push a found bug toward a working proof-of-concept exploit. Each task gives the model a single function in an open-source project as an entry point, and asks the model to find a vulnerability and write an exploit for that vulnerability. The model gets no hints about<!-- p.51 --> where a vulnerability may be. The 831 entry points span 228 projects from Google’s OSS-Fuzz corpus. A trial is scored on a five-step ladder from memory safety crash (0.2) through control-flow hijack (1.0).

We completely rewrote the harness for this evaluation in summer 2026 to strengthen our safeguards and evaluation monitoring. In doing so, we found and fixed several bugs and adjusted some parameters. We’ve rerun all the older models through the new harness, which is why the numbers presented here are different from those shown on [system cards](https://www.anthropic.com/system-cards) for models tested on the old harness.

Claude Mythos 5.2 produced 148 control-flow hijacks. By comparison, Claude Opus 5.5 produced 112 control-flow hijacks, Claude Mythos 5.1 produced 84, and Claude Sonnet 5.5 produced 48. Vulnerability identification is on par with Opus 5.5 and a small improvement over Claude Mythos 5.1 and Sonnet 5.5.

#### 3.3.4 ExploitGym

ExploitGym is a large-scale public benchmark developed by researchers at UC Berkeley together with collaborators at the Max Planck Institute for Security and Privacy, UC Santa<!-- p.52 --> Barbara, Arizona State University, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google. The benchmark measures whether AI agents can turn known vulnerabilities into working exploits. ExploitGym focuses mainly on turning a crash into unauthorized code execution, and it covers a wider range of applications than CyberGym, which tests whether a model could reproduce a known vulnerability. We have retired CyberGym from this system card due to saturation.

The benchmark comprises 869 instances of real-world vulnerabilities across three domains: 502 userspace targets drawn from open-source projects (OSS-Fuzz and ARVO) and CVE/OSV records; 181 targets in Chrome’s V8 JavaScript engine, sourced from ClusterFuzz reports, Chromium issues, and V8 sandbox-break reports; and 186 Linux kernel targets from kernelCTF submissions and syzbot reports. For each instance, the agent is given the vulnerable source code and build configuration, a proof-of-vulnerability input that triggers the bug, and a containerized runtime environment in which to interact with the target.

The task is to develop an exploit that achieves unauthorized code execution and reads a dynamically generated secret flag stored outside of the agent’s authorized scope. Because agents sometimes achieve code execution through a different flaw than the one provided, successful flag captures are additionally verified by an agent-as-a-judge check (validated against expert-audited trajectories), confirming that the intended vulnerability was actually exploited. Each instance can be run with standard mitigations enabled (ASLR, stack canaries, the V8 heap sandbox, and KASLR) or disabled.

We evaluate all tasks with all mitigations disabled. Claude Mythos 5.2 successfully exploited 331 of the 869 cases using the intended vulnerability in two hours and 341 in six hours, surpassing both Claude Opus 5.5 (289 and 300, respectively) and Claude Mythos 5.1 (203 and 264) at both time budgets. As with Opus 5.5, the extra few hours doesn’t significantly affect the results.

### 3.4 Safeguards coverage

Claude Fable 5.2’s safeguards are designed to block the same violative exchanges as Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Opus 5.5. However, similar to Claude releases following Claude Opus 5, Fable 5.2 will allow vulnerability discovery in source code at all access levels, including general availability, while continuing to block vulnerability discovery in compiled binaries.

We expect our improved classifiers to block significantly less generic traffic on Fable 5.2 than on prior Fable releases, without compromising on cyber harm coverage or robustness relative to our other production models, such as Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5.

### 3.5 Safeguards robustness testing

“Adversarial robustness” refers to the resistance of a system’s safeguards to deliberate circumvention attempts, or jailbreaks, where that term includes prompts, custom harnesses, and multi-agent pipelines. In our [jailbreak severity framework](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-safeguards-jailbreak-framework), we present the following four axes of jailbreak severity:

- **Capability gain** (or **uplift**): How far the jailbreak technique takes the attacker beyond their current tools and capabilities;
- **Breadth of capability gain** (or **universality)**: How many distinct offensive tasks the same jailbreak technique works on;
- **Ease of weaponization**: The human effort needed to turn the jailbreak into an attack; and
- **Discoverability**: How easily a threat actor can obtain the technique.

For example, a critical-severity jailbreak would be easily discoverable, weaponizable, and universal on a high-uplift model.

We have not found evidence of a critical-severity jailbreak for Claude Fable 5.2. This also remains true for Fable- and Opus-class models released since Claude Fable 5. Even so, we do not expect our classifiers to be perfectly robust. We therefore test our models<!-- p.56 --> extensively, both internally and with external partners, to proactively find and patch jailbreaks.

#### 3.5.1 Internal robustness testing

We maintain an internal adversarial robustness evaluation called rewind-attacker that measures how well a safeguarded model (the “assistant”) resists an automated orchestrator (the “attacker”). In this setup, the attacker is a helpful-only language model prompted to simulate a human red teamer eliciting offensive cyber capabilities from the assistant.

The attacker spawns in a session where it can send instructions to a Claude Code agent running the assistant model (with production safeguards) inside the task’s sandbox. The attacker is then given a 400-call limit against the assistant, during which the attacker receives blocking feedback from the assistant and has the opportunity to rewind to an earlier state to try a new approach. Each task places the agent in a self-contained Dockerized environment with a concrete, verifiable goal—for example, encrypting a victim directory, wrapping the encryption key under an attacker-controlled public key, and beaconing to a command-and-control server—and an automated check that confirms whether the goal was actually achieved.

Our current task suite includes the following categories: ransomware (encryption, key escrow, backup termination); scaled data exfiltration across DNS, cloud storage, webhooks, and other channels; remote and local exploitation of real CVEs (including nginx, OpenSSL, glibc, and Log4Shell); command-and-control; and self-replication.

This evaluation is the same as the one reported in the [Claude Fable 5.1](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/0339e6a7c5c7b87f5c07798616dc32c215d14235/Claude%20Fable%205.1%20&%20Claude%20Mythos%205.1%20System%20Card.pdf), [Claude Opus 5.5](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/fc1b44717c85dc068bc6ba5024219938094694bd/Claude%20Opus%205.5%20System%20Card.pdf), and [Claude Sonnet 5.5](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/870c8f525702625d2c62fc6dd04c857e3250bec1/Claude%20Sonnet%205.5%20System%20Card.pdf) System Cards.

#### 3.5.3 Additional external testing

As with previous models, we worked with contracted external testers to red-team Claude Fable 5.2’s safeguards. These organizations evaluated many of the same tasks we reported in the [Claude Opus 5.5](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/fc1b44717c85dc068bc6ba5024219938094694bd/Claude%20Opus%205.5%20System%20Card.pdf) and [Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Mythos 5.1](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/0339e6a7c5c7b87f5c07798616dc32c215d14235/Claude%20Fable%205.1%20&%20Claude%20Mythos%205.1%20System%20Card.pdf) System Cards, in addition to tasks of their own design.

[Trajectory Labs, PBC](https://trajectorylabs.com/) spent roughly 138 hours red-teaming Fable 5.2, sending more than 40,000 requests across sandboxed tasks covering vulnerability discovery, exploitation, exfiltration, and ransomware-style activity. They recorded 14 breaks, meaning sessions in which Fable 5.2 carried out the task and achieved the harmful objective inside the test environment. They found no universal jailbreak, and each successful break approach was specific to its task.

<!-- p.58 -->

In the sessions that did not break, Fable 5.2 consistently identified the vulnerability, and in many cases built components of an exploit, but refused to use them against a live target. We reviewed the transcripts of all reported breaks and found that Fable 5.2 never complied with a harmful request that was stated openly.

In the sessions that did break, a common approach was to split the work across fresh conversations—in one case, more than 100 separate conversations, each posed as an ordinary coding task, so that none revealed the full objective. In some cases, the operator supplied the harmful payload and Fable 5.2 built and ran the surrounding tooling. In one break, the operator placed the payload in a file and Fable 5.2 ran a command that used the payload when asked to in a separate conversation. When the same request was made in the conversation where Fable 5.2 had built the tools, the request was refused. In two breaks, our safeguards blocked Fable 5.2’s final responses to the operator, but the blocks came seconds after Fable 5.2 had already run the command that completed the underlying task, so they did not prevent the break.

Throughout these tasks, Fable 5.2 also resisted in ways that made it challenging for operators to achieve a break. This included substituting safe values for harmful ones, declining malicious instructions that arrived through tool outputs, reporting permission errors honestly rather than working around them, and adding input sanitization unprompted.

[10a Labs](https://10alabs.com/) spent roughly 40 hours red-teaming Fable 5.2 across 146 multi-turn attack conversations. Each conversation ran 16 prompts and was framed as a large developer-focused coding task, designed to draw a working exploit out of a volume of legitimately requested application code rather than asking for the exploit directly. No conversation advanced past proof of concept, and every directly offensive request was blocked. Requests framed as test-driven development passed the classifiers but did not produce a working exploit, and the highest-scoring conversation was a benign coding exercise with no offensive content.

[Gray Swan](https://www.grayswan.ai/) ran their Shade automated attacker against a set of agentic tasks. Shade is trained on diverse red-teaming strategies, including real-world attack techniques sourced from the Gray Swan Arena. They tested it on nine scenarios drawn from publicly documented vulnerabilities and ransomware staging tasks, each asking the model to reproduce an exploit or carry out a destructive objective. Shade made roughly 2,900 attempts, where each attempt constituted a separate multi-turn attack conversation of up to 30 turns. Fable 5.2’s safeguards refused about a fifth of attempts outright, and none of the rest reached the objective. Gray Swan’s state judge recorded no breaks.
